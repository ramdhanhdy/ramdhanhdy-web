## Context

A fictional 100-store Indonesian coffee chain ("Kopi Senja") runs three seasonal
limited-time-offer campaigns simultaneously. During each campaign, things go
wrong: stores receive wrong quantities, confirmations go missing, photo proofs
are never uploaded, setups happen late. These operational exceptions pile up
fast — 1,603 of them across three campaigns — and nobody can tell which ones
matter, who should fix them, or whether the fixes actually worked.

## Approach

The system layers four analytical stages:

1. **Detection** — 9 exception rules across allocation, dispatch, confirmation,
   photo proof, and sales data. Each exception is tagged with severity, SLA
   window, and business impact.
2. **Ranking** — A priority score formula (severity weight × 100 + age pressure
   + business impact + campaign urgency) produces a daily action list.
3. **Diagnosis** — A 4-layer statistical insight engine: Pareto/Gini
   concentration, ANOVA/Kruskal-Wallis group comparisons, binomial/permutation
   tests, and rebalancing/financial modeling.
4. **Verification** — Matched comparison between stores that received
   intervention and statistically similar stores that didn't. The intervention
   effect is injected into simulated data with realistic noise (8% failures,
   5% assignment errors, 5% reopen), and the methodology must recover it.

## Evidence

- 1,603 exceptions detected across 100 stores and 3 campaigns
- 367 tests passing (unit, integration, methodology validation)
- Gini coefficient of 0.459 — exceptions concentrate in a subset of stores
- Intervention recovery: 60% resolve rate vs 34% control (26pp effect,
  statistically significant, injected effect was 30pp)
- Dashboard cut from 514 lines to ~200, organized as question-named views

## Outcome

The deliverable is a decision memo, not just a dashboard. The methodology
survives noise — failures, reopens, and null results — rather than assuming
every intervention succeeds. The circularity (simulated effect, simulated
recovery) is disclosed as a feature: the measurement approach is the product,
not the finding.
